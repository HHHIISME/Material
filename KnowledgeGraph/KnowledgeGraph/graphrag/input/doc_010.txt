# Li-2014-Fabrication of a novel Ti-TiO2-ZrO2 fi

Fabrication of a novel Ti–TiO2–ZrO2 ﬁber for solid-
phase microextraction followed by high-
performance liquid chromatography for sensitive
determination of UV ﬁlters in environmental water
samples†
Yi Li,a Yaoxia Yang,a Haixia Liu,a Xuemei Wangab and Xinzhen Du*ab
A novel Ti–TiO2–ZrO2 ﬁber for solid-phase microextraction (SPME) was fabricated by chemical oxidization
of a Ti wire substrate and subsequent electrodeposition of ZrO2 nanoparticle coating. The chemically
oxidized procedure aﬀorded in situ fabrication of porous TiO2 nanostructures on a Ti wire and provided
a desirable substrate for further deposition of ZrO2 nanoparticle coating. The best extraction capability
was obtained by ZrO2 nanoparticles with a size distribution of 20–30 nm and a coating thickness of
about 4.5 mm after 10 cyclic voltammetry scans. The main parameters aﬀecting the Ti–TiO2–ZrO2 ﬁber
for SPME of ultraviolet (UV) ﬁlters were investigated coupled to high-performance liquid chromatography
with UV detection. A good linearity was achieved in the range of 0.5–500 mg L1 with correlation
coeﬃcients higher than 0.996. Limits of detection were 0.032–0.082 mg L1 (S/N ¼ 3). The single ﬁber
repeatability ranged from 4.3% to 12% and the ﬁber-to-ﬁber reproducibility ranged from 4.3% to 11% for
SPME of spiked water with 50 mg L1 UV ﬁlters (n ¼ 5). Furthermore this ﬁber has high stability, long
service time and high extraction capability for UV ﬁlters compared to the commercially available
polymeric ﬁbers.
1.
Introduction
Solid phase microextraction (SPME) is an attractive alternative
to traditional sample preparation methods because it integrates
sample extraction, concentration and introduction into one
step.1 It has been successfully applied to environmental,2–5
biological,6 crude oil7 and food analysis8 coupled with gas
chromatography (GC) or high performance liquid chromatog-
raphy (HPLC). However, commercial silica-based bers are
relatively expensive and show some drawbacks such as fragility,
bending of the needle, the stripping of coatings, and easily
swelling in an organic solvent which limit its widespread
applications.9 Therefore, the development of novel micro-
extraction bers has become popular in this eld in the past two
decades.10 Great eﬀorts have been directed to the development
of metal-based bers with thermally and chemically stable
coatings that have high extraction performance and/or selec-
tivity for the specied analytes.11–20 With regard to inorganic
materials, ZrO2 is one of the most versatile ceramic materials
due to its durability, resistance to corrosion and wear, strong
adhesion onto metallic surfaces and non-toxicity.21 It has been
successfully used as a stationary phase in HPLC to analyze many
analytes due to its high mechanical stability, good adsorption
and excellent pH stability.22 Budziak et al. also has carried out
the electrodeposition of ZrO2 on a NiTi wire as a ber for SPME
of halophenols in water samples.23 This novel NiTi–ZrO2 ber
showed a better extraction capability in comparison with the
commercial bers and a lifetime of over 300 extraction cycles.
Thus, it is a promising alternative for low-cost analysis due to its
robustness and easy preparation.
Recently, nanostructured TiO2-based SPME bers were
fabricated through in situ oxidation of Ti wires with hydrogen
peroxide24 and anodization of Ti wires in an electrolyte con-
taining ethylene glycol and ammonium uoride.25 These novel
TiO2-based
SPME
bers
provide
promising
alternative
substrates for further fabrication of highly eﬃcient and rigid
ZrO2 coatings because they exhibit a much larger surface area
and better active sites. Electrodeposition can oﬀer a rigid
control of coating thickness, uniformity and deposition rate for
ZrO2 coatings. This technique is especially attractive for further
deposition onto the substrates with complex shapes.21

aromatic structures conjugated with carbon–carbon double

2.
Experimental
2.1
Materials and reagents
Ti wire (purity 99.9%, f0.127 mm) was obtained from Alfa Aesar
(Ward Hill, MA, USA). A 0.45 mm micropore membrane of pol-
yvinylidene uoride was supplied by Xingya Purifying Material
Factory (Shanghai, China). Hydrouoric acid (HF), hydrochloric
acid (HCl), nitric acid (HNO3) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2,
30%, w/v) were obtained from Yantai Shuangshuang Chemicals
Ltd (Shandong, China). Sodium chloride (NaCl) and potassium
chloride (KCl) were obtained from Shanghai Hunter Fine
Chemicals Ltd (Shanghai, China). Zirconium(IV) oxychloride
octahydrate (ZrOCl2$8H2O) was purchased from Sinopharm
Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The HPLC-grade
methanol was purchased from Yuwang Chemical Company
(Shandong,
China).
Certied
2-hydroxy-4-methoxy-
benzophenone (BP-3, purity 100%, lot no. 14971), 2-ethylhexyl-
4-methoxycinnamate (EHMC, purity 98%, lot no. 18565) and 2-
ethylhexyl 4-(N,N-dimethylamino) benzoate (OD-PABA, purity
97%, lot no. 23111) were purchased from AccuStandard (New
Haven, USA). 2-Ethylhexyl salicylate (EHS, purity 99%, lot no.
00902) was obtained from Dr Ehrenstorfer (Augsburg, Ger-
many). The stock standard solution of 100 mg L1 of each
compound was prepared in methanol and stored in amber
bottles in the refrigerator at 4 C, shielding from light. Working
standard solutions were prepared by diluting the stock standard
solution with ultrapure water to the required concentration to
study extraction performance under diﬀerent conditions. All
other reagents were of analytical grade.
2.2
Instruments
A Waters 600E multi-solvent delivery system (Milford, MA, USA)
equipped with Waters 2487 dual l absorbance detector and a
Waters Sunre C18 chromatographic column (150 mm 
4.6 mm, 5 mm) was used for all separation processes. Data
collection was obtained with a N2000 workstation (Zhejiang
University, China). The mobile phase was methanol–water (90/
10, v/v) and the wavelength of UV detection was set at 310 nm.
Desorption was performed in a commercially available SPME-
HPLC interface (Supelco, PA, USA) and sealed by polyether ether
ketone septum which is tight enough to withstand a pressure as
high as 29.7 MPa. Ultrapure water was obtained from a Sudreli
water purifying system (Chongqing, China). Heating and stir-
ring were carried out in a DF-101S water bath with a magnetic
stirrer (Zhengzhou, China). Electrodeposition of ZrO2 nano-
particle coating onto the surface of the TiO2-based wire was
performed using a CHI832D analyzer (Shanghai, China). The
morphology and composition of the fabricated bers were
characterized by using an Ultra Plus microscope (Zeiss, Ober-
kochen, Germany) equipped with semi-quantitative micro-
analysis by energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS).
2.3
Fabrication of Ti–TiO2–ZrO2 bers
The fabrication of Ti–TiO2–ZrO2 bers includes the following
steps. A 10 mm tip segment of Ti wires was soaked in a mixture
of concentrated HF, concentrated HNO3 and water (1 : 3 : 6, v/v/v)
at room temperature for 30 s, followed by ultrasonication in
water.24 Thereaer, the etching part was dipped in H2O2 (30%,
w/v) solution held in a 15 mL plastic test tube sealed with a cap
and then the plastic test tube was placed in an oil bath (200 r
min1, 80 C) for 24 h. Finally the Ti wires were rinsed gently
with water and allowed to dry in air. Prior to electrodeposition
of ZrO2, the Ti–TiO2 bers were ultrasonically cleaned in
ultrapure water for 1 min to remove surface debris and dipped
in a 1.0 mol L1 sodium hydroxide for 60 min activation of the
TiO2-based surface and then in 0.1 mol L1 HCl for 30 min, and
washed with ultrapure water for 5 min. Subsequently ZrO2
nanoparticles were deposited onto the TiO2-based surface in the
electrolyte of 0.005 mol L1 ZrOCl2 and 0.1 mol L1 KCl by CV
between 2.0 and 0 V (versus Ag/AgCl) at a scanning rate of
20 mV s1 for 10 cycles.36 The as-fabricated bers were gently
rinsed with ultrapure water and then dried in air.

wastewater samples. River water samples were freshly collected
from diﬀerent sites in the Lanzhou section of the Yellow River.
Wastewater samples were collected from local wastewater
treatment plants. All real water samples were collected in amber
glass, ltered through 0.45 mm micropore membranes, and
then stored in the dark at 4 C. The pH value of water samples

2.5
SPME-HPLC procedure
15 mL of working standard solution or sample solution was
transferred into a 20 mL glass vial with a 1 cm magnetic stirrer
bar inside a Teon septum. The Ti–TiO2–ZrO2 ber was
immersed into the stirred solution for 30 min at 35 C. Subse-
quently, the ber was withdrawn from the sample solution and
introduced into the SPME-HPLC interface for static desorption
in the mobile-phase. Aer desorption, a six-port valve was
switched from load to inject position; the mobile phase was
passed through the interface and UV lters were introduced
into the analytical column at a ow rate of 1 mL min1 for HPLC
analysis. For next extraction, the ber was immersed into
methanol and ultrapure water to eliminate possible carry-over
for 10 min and 5 min, respectively.
3.
Results and discussion
3.1
Characterization of nanostructured Ti–TiO2–ZrO2 bers
Nanostructured ZrO2 can be deposited onto the TiO2-based
surface through cathodic reactions.37 The thickness of ZrO2
coating can be controlled by the number of CV cycles. Fig. 1
shows the micrographs of scanning electron microscopy (SEM)
obtained for a Ti wire ber at diﬀerent stages. The TiO2-based
surface (Fig. 1a and b) is rough and porous with a pore size of
50–120 nm compared to that of the bare Ti wire (Fig. 1a1) and
can provide a desirable substrate for subsequent compact
electrodeposition of ZrO2. As shown in Fig. 1c–f, some crackings
of the electrodeposited ZrO2 coatings were observed. These
crackings result from a tension due to shrinking of the ZrO2
coating layers.38 Thicker ZrO2 coatings electrodeposited by 20
CV cycles and 30 CV cycles lead to more signicant crackings as
shown in Fig. 1e and f. This phenomenon results in a signicant
decrease in the eﬀective surface area of ZrO2 coatings and
thereby a great decrease in the peak area, and the best extrac-
tion capability was obtained for ZrO2 coating with 10 CV cycles
(see Fig. S1†). Furthermore higher magnication of ZrO2
coating in Fig. 1d1 clearly demonstrates that ZrO2 nanoparticles
were fabricated onto the nanostructured TiO2 surface with a
size distribution of 20–30 nm and a thickness of about 4.5 mm,
and thereby greatly increasing the extraction eﬃciency of target
UV lters. On the one hand, the electrodeposited ZrO2 nano-
particle coating exhibits strong aﬃnity for polar organic
compounds due to the presence of Zr–OH at the surface of ZrO2
coating. On the other hand, it is expected that strong Lewis
acidic sites at the surface of ZrO2 nanoparticles would show
good aﬃnity for target analytes with a Lewis basic character.
Thus 10 CV cycles were employed for the electrodeposition of
ZrO2 coating in this study.
The chemical analysis of the TiO2-based wire was also per-
formed by EDS. The spectrum obtained for the TiO2-based
surface only shows peaks corresponding to the presence of
titanium and oxygen (Fig. 2a). For the Ti–TiO2–ZrO2 ber, a
strong peak also appears at 2.1 keV (Fig. 2b). This can be
attributed to the emission line of zirconium due to ZrO2
nanoparticles electrodeposited onto the TiO2-based surface.
These phenomena provide additional evidence for the results
obtained by SEM.
3.2
Optimization of SPME conditions
The main parameters aﬀecting the Ti–TiO2–ZrO2 ber for SPME
of four UV lters were examined in aqueous samples. Depen-
dence of SPME on the extraction and desorption times,
temperature, ionic strength, stirring rate and pH was optimized
to obtain an excellent extraction eﬃciency.

extraction time is a crucial factor in SPME. Generally the longer

nearly reaches its maximum within 30 min except OD-PABA.

extracted UV lters was performed in the mobile phase. For all
UV lters, the peak area reached the constant maximum within
4 min. Thus, 4 min was employed for desorption of UV lters in
the mobile phase.
3.2.2
Eﬀect of the extraction temperature. The temperature
is a very important parameter for SPME because it can aﬀect the
extraction rate and equilibrium. Fig. 3b presents the eﬀect of
the temperature on the extraction of UV lters from 15 to 65 C.
The highest extraction eﬃciencies were obtained at 35 C.
Thereaer, the extraction capability quickly decreased when the
temperature continuously increased. This is most probably due
to the decreased distribution coeﬃcients of UV lters between
the ZrO2 coating and water along with increasing temperature.
In addition, the dissolution of UV lters also decreases the
extraction eﬃciency at higher temperature. Therefore an
extraction temperature of 35 C was used.
3.2.3
Eﬀect of the ionic strength. Addition of the salt can
decrease the solubility of less polar organic analytes and aﬀect
the surface properties of ber coatings at the same time, and
nally inuence the adsorption behavior of the prepared bers.
Thus the eﬀect of the ionic strength was further examined by
adding NaCl ranging from 0 to 30% (w/v) into the working
solutions. A negative eﬀect on extraction eﬃciencies of UV
lters was observed in the aqueous phase with more than NaCl
of 5%. Therefore, no salt addition was recommended in this
study.
3.2.4
Eﬀect of the stirring rate. The extraction eﬃciency
enhances with increasing stirring rate because stirring accel-
erates the mass transfer of the analytes from the solution to
ber coating. In the experiment, the extraction eﬃciency
maximum of UV lters was achieved at the stirring rate of 800 r
min1. More vigorous stirring led to bubble formation which is
unfavorable to the adsorption process at the surface of the ber
with Ti–TiO2–ZrO2 nanoparticle coating. Therefore the stirring
rate was xed at 800 r min1.
3.2.5
Eﬀect of the pH. The eﬀect of the pH on the SPME of
UV lters was examined within the pH range of 2.0–9.0. Due to
their protonation at pH < 4, all UV lters are capable of existing
in their cationic form and soluble in aqueous solutions. Thus
extremely
low
extraction
eﬃciencies
for
UV
lters
were
obtained. At pH 7.0, their neutral forms are benecial for their
adsorption onto the Ti–TiO2–ZrO2 coating and thereby the
highest extraction eﬃciencies were achieved. In the case of pH >
7.0, however, all UV lters exhibit rapidly decreased extraction
eﬃciencies. Therefore, pH 7.0 was employed for the SPME
procedure.

Comparison of the proposed method with reported

The proposed method was compared with the reported
methods with respect to the sample volume, extraction time,
linear range, LOD, RSD and recovery. As can be seen in Table 3,
the extraction equilibrium was almost achieved within 30 min.
Moreover, the LOD values also suggest that this proposed
method exhibits high sensitivity for the determination of tar-
geted UV lters. A simple operation procedure makes the
sample preparation easy, rapid and suitable for the pre-
concentration and determination of trace UV lters in real
environmental water. The experimental results for the proposed
method are comparable or better than those in the reported
literatures.31,33,35,39–41
4.
Conclusions
In this study, ZrO2 nanoparticle coating was fabricated on a
nanostructured TiO2-based substrate as a SPME ber coating by
previous chemical oxidation and subsequent electrodeposition
using a CV technique. The ZrO2 nanoparticle coatings were
tightly attached onto the nanostructured TiO2 substrate with a
larger surface area. This robust ber was more eﬀective than the
commercially available PDMS and PA bers for SPME of UV
lters and used for more than 250 extraction and desorption
cycles without the loss of the extraction capability. This novel
Ti–TiO2–ZrO2 ber was easily fabricated in a highly reproduc-
ible manner. It oﬀered a simple, rapid, sensitive and inexpen-
sive
pretreatment
way
for
the
preconcentration
and
determination of targeted UV lters in real environmental water
samples.
