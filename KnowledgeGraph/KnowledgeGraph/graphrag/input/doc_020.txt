ABSTRACT
The highly ﬂexible ZrO2/C nanoﬁber ﬁlm was fabricated by electrospinning
followed by heat treatment of stabilization and carbonization. The effects of
carbonization temperature on the morphology, microstructure, and perfor-
mance of ZrO2/C nanoﬁber ﬁlm were investigated. As the temperature
increases, the tetragonal phase of the ZrO2 nanoparticles gradually transformed
into monoclinic phase. As a result, the mechanical and electrical properties of
the ZrO2/C nanoﬁber ﬁlm were improved. When the temperature reached
1100 C, the ZrO2/C nanoﬁber ﬁlm exhibited the best ﬂexibility with the ﬂexural
modulus of 6.24 ± 0.08 MPa, the highest conductivity of 476.5 S/m, and
promising electrocatalytic activity. These improvements can be ascribed to the
phase transition toughening effect of ZrO2 nanoparticles, the increase in oxygen
vacancy concentration in ZrO2, and the improvement in graphitization of carbon
matrix. Hence, the ZrO2/C nanoﬁber ﬁlm served as a counter electrode for
ﬂexible dye-sensitized solar cells, and an efﬁciency of 2.97% was achieved.
Introduction
The carbon nanoﬁbers (CNFs) with free-standing,
good

zirconia nanoparticles (ZrO2 NPs) were incorporated
into CNFs matrix. The obtained ZrO2/C composite
nanoﬁber ﬁlm exhibited excellent ﬂexibility, whereas
its electrical property was poor.

Experimental
Fabrication of ZrO2/C composite nanoﬁber
ﬁlm
According to our previous work [16], the ZrO2/C
nanoﬁber ﬁlm is fabricated by electrospinning and
heat treatment. Brieﬂy, PAN (0.4 g) and PVP (0.4 g)
were dissolved in DMF (6 ml) at room temperature,
with mechanical stirring for 3 h. Then, Zr(C3H7O)4
(0.72 ml) and acetic acid (0.3 ml) were added to the
solution with continuous stirring. The PAN/PVP/
Zr(C3H7O)4 solution was electrospun on a grounded
collector to obtain PAN/PVP/Zr(C3H7O)4 nanoﬁber
ﬁlm. During electrospinning, a ﬂow rate is 1.1 ml/h
and the high voltage is 15 kV. The obtained PAN/
PVP/Zr(C3H7O)4 nanoﬁber ﬁlm was pre-oxidized at
270 C in air for 2 h with a heating rate of 2 C/min
and then carbonized at different temperatures (800,
900, 1000, and 1100 C) in nitrogen for 1 h. Finally,
the ZrO2/C nanoﬁber ﬁlms are obtained and labeled
as ZCN-800, ZCN-900, ZCN-1000, and ZCN-1100,
respectively. As a comparison, CNFs ﬁlm from PAN/
PVP solution was also prepared with the same
treatments.
Assembling FDSSCs
The assembly process is illustrated in Fig. 1. First, the
photoanodes were fabricated according to Horim’s
work [23, 24]. 0.1 g TiO2 was added in 1 ml anhy-
drous ethanol, and the mixture was stirred for 4 h
followed by ultrasound for 0.5 h. The obtained sus-
pension was electrospray deposited on the ITO/PET
(13 X/sq, Peccell). The TiO2 layer with ITO/PET was
stored at 150 C for 6 h. Subsequently, all the TiO2
photoanodes were immersed in 0.5 mM N719 dye
solution for 24 h. The anodes were washed with
absolute ethanol to remove the unanchored dyes. The
FDSSCs were assembled by TiO2 photoanode and
ﬂexible ZrO2/C nanoﬁber ﬁlm CE with quasi-solid
electrolyte
(OPV-MPV-I)
using
a
60-lm
sealing
spacer (Surlyn 1702, DuPont). And the active area of
the cells was 0.25 cm-2.

Results and discussion
Morphology and microstructure
of composite nanoﬁbers
The phase structures of the CNFs and ZrO2/C
nanoﬁbers were characterized by XRD. As displayed
in Fig. 2a, the main diffraction peak at 24.2 can be
indexed to the (002) plane of graphite structure
[25, 26]. In addition, the patterns in Fig. 2b–e illus-
trate the structural changes of ZrO2/C nanoﬁbers
(ZCN-800, ZCN-900, ZCN-1000 and ZCN-1100). As
shown in Fig. 2b, the ZCN-800 possesses prominent
diffraction peaks at 30.2, 50.4, and 60.7, which are
assigned to (011), (112), and (121) planes of T-ZrO2
(No. 50-1089 JCPDS card), respectively [27]. By
comparison, a few new peaks appear for ZCN-900 in
Fig. 2c. These new peaks locate at 28.1, 31.3, 34.2,
39.3, and 55.4, belonging to the (-111), (111), (200),
(220), and (031) reﬂections of M-ZrO2 (No. 37-1484
JCPDS card), respectively [27–29]. It indicates that the
monoclinic phase of ZrO2 transforms from tetragonal
at 900 C. As the temperature further increases
(1000–1100 C), the intensity of diffraction peaks
corresponding to M-ZrO2 becomes stronger (Fig. 2d,
e). In the meanwhile, the intensity of peaks of T-ZrO2
becomes weaker. Especially for ZCN-1100, the peaks
assigned to (-112), (031) planes of M-ZrO2 appear. In
general, ZrO2 NPs are stable in the form of mono-
clinic phase at room temperature. However, the sta-
bility of T-ZrO2 can be greatly improved due to the
presence of defects and impurities in ZrO2 NPs. After
cooling down, the T-ZrO2 in ZrO2/C nanoﬁber can be
obtained. However, as the temperature increases, the
defects and impurities are reduced. The T-ZrO2
would gradually transform into M-ZrO2. And the
T-ZrO2 and M-ZrO2 can coexist in ZrO2/C nanoﬁ-
bers. Besides, with the increase in temperature, the
proportion of monoclinic phase increases and the
tetragonal
phase

The surface compositions and electronic structures
of ZrO2/C nanoﬁbers were measured by XPS tech-
nique. Figure 4a shows full XPS spectra scanned from
0 to 1300 eV, which provides both a compositional
overview and information about main elemental
components in ZCN-1000 and ZCN-1100. All samples
produce the C1s and O1s peak at about 284.4 and
529.3 eV, respectively. A major difference among
them was the peaks intensity of the Zr and O. It is
obvious that the peak intensities increase with the
increase in carbonization temperature, implying an
increase in the surface concentrations of Zr and O
[31].
To further investigate the state of these atoms,
high-resolution XPS spectra of Zr, C, and O were
analyzed. The Zr 3d spectra region is shown in
Fig. 4b. These spectra are composed of two primary
peaks, which can be assigned to Zr 3d5/2 and Zr 3d3/2,
respectively [32, 33]. By contrast, an increase of about
0.3 eV in the binding energy of Zr 3d of ZCN-1100
can be found. This shift is ascribed to the increased
oxygen vacancy concentration. Hence, the ZrO2
attracts outer electrons of Zr atom strongly, resulting
in an increase in binding energy. Figure 4c demon-
strates the C1s high-resolution spectra, which reveal
two carbon components of samples. The main carbon
peak locates at 284.7 eV which corresponds to the
amorphous carbon, and the other peak at 285.3 eV
may be assigned to carboxyl carbon. The O1s spectra
region is depicted in Fig. 4d. There are three kinds of
oxygen states for two samples: Zr–O, O–H, and C–O.
Obviously, the binding energy of O–H and the
intensity of C–O were changed. An increase in
binding energy of O–H is attributed to the fact that
the -OH group is absorbed to balance the charge
change
caused
by
oxygen
vacancy.
And
the
decrease in intensity of C–O is due to the further
decomposition of organic compounds in elevated
temperature.

Mechanical and electrical properties
Assuredly, the structural changes have a signiﬁcant
effect on the performance of the ZrO2/C nanoﬁber
ﬁlm. Accordingly, the effects of carbonization tem-
perature on electrical and mechanical properties were
studied.

further evaluate electrocatalytic abilities of various
ﬁlms by using the symmetric cells. In general, the
Tafel curve can be divided into three parts: the lower

higher voltage, and the Tafel zone in the middle of
steep slope (which determines the catalytic properties
of the electrode) [37]. It can be observed that the
limiting diffusion current density (Jlim) increases as
the temperature increases. On the other hand, the
exchange current density (J0) calculated from the
intersection of the linear cathodic and anodic Tafel
polarization curves shows similar trend like Jlim.
Moreover, the J0 value of a ﬁlm is inversely propor-
tional to charge transfer resistance (RCT-Tafel) using
the relation:

onset of the ﬁrst semicircle, the ﬁrst semicircle arises

As seen, the ZCN-1100 CE possesses the smaller RS

activity. It may also be noted that ZCN-1100 CE

Obviously, the electrocatalytic properties of ZrO2/
C nanoﬁbers are greatly improved with the increase
in carbonization temperature. It can be ascribed to the
slightly increase in oxygen vacancy concentration.
These oxygen vacancies can be used as the diffusion
channels of O2- to effectively improved conductivity,
increased catalytic active sites [22, 41]. Of course, the
improvement in graphitization of carbon matrix is
one of the factors that cannot be ignored.

become a recombination center, leading to the gen-
eration of dark current [43]. Thus, the photoanode
should to be further optimized.
